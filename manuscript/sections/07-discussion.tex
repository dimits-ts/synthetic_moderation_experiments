\section{Conclusion}

We identified a new research direction that recently came to be with the discovery of LLM emergent social abilities, which we termed \acf{sdg}. This direction has been implicitly used for experimentation on a variety of domains. We analyzed interdisciplinary literature and created a theoretical framework that can support synthetic simulations for a variety of tasks and domains. Specifically, we identified four main design principles and used them to define an evaluation suite and general-purpose components that are required for \ac{sdg}; namely, crafting LLM personas and executing a dynamic discussion. Our analysis suggests that careful instruction-prompting can induce persistent, toxic behavior to safety-aligned LLMs using only conversational context. We also found that LLM users are incapable of choosing not to speak, and that using LLM personas improves the alignment of content variety of synthetic discussions with that of human discussions. 

While recent studies predominantly rely on proprietary LLMs for simulations, we argue that this choice is both excessive in terms of the required capabilities and unscalable, mainly due to high inference costs. To support this position, we demonstrated that experiments using small open-source models can reduce inference costs by more than forty-four times compared to middle-capability, proprietary models, through an adapted cost-comparison methodology. Despite their small size, our models were capable enough to replicate interesting, emergent social behaviors such as weaving personal stories and experiences to support their positions, being sensitive to overt interventions from authority figures, taking collective action against antagonistic users, or sometimes defending them against perceived injustice on the part of the facilitator.

Despite ongoing skepticism regarding the use and generalizability of LLM simulations, we showed that \ac{sdg} systems can yield meaningful insights by uncovering a critical limitation of LLM facilitators: \emph{their tendency to constantly intervene in discussions}. Furthermore, we demonstrated that different facilitation strategies significantly influence the patterns of facilitation observed. Overall, our work represents a methodological shift in the design and execution of \ac{sdg} experiments. Our contributions include:
(1) a theoretical framework for designing, documenting, and evaluating \ac{sdg} systems,
(2) a cost-comparison methodology for social science experiments,
(3) an exploration of available models and algorithms,
(4) inherent limitations and a promising direction in LLM facilitation, and
(5) a large-scale dataset of synthetic discussions. 


\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Limitations}
Our research is fundamentally limited by the lack of robust evaluation metrics for synthetic text. While the diversity metric used throughout this study represents a meaningful step forward, we acknowledge that content variety is only a heuristic for measuring similarity to human discussion. More broadly, the field of \ac{gabm} still lacks reliable evaluation strategies that do not rely on extensive, specialized, and costly human annotation. All things considered, our self-imposed restriction of not using human participants and evaluators (a core motivation for our work) has forced us to experiment with automated heuristics, which may not be as convincing as extensive (and extremely expensive) human involvement.

Our simulations are further constrained by the stereotypical and repetitive patterns of speech that many instruction-tuned models exhibit. The scale of our ablation experiments also prevents us from benchmarking proprietary models (\S\ref{sec:scalability:cost}; Table~\ref{tab:cost}), although we have shown that small models are sufficient for our simulations. Finally, while our study focuses on monetary cost as the primary constraint faced by researchers, other factors such as computational speed and ease of use should also be considered in future work. 


\subsection{Future work}
Future work should focus on developing more robust methods for evaluating synthetic discussions and replicating social behaviors using LLMs. This includes exploring additional ways to quantify the similarity between human and LLM-generated discussions, as well as establishing standardized benchmarks to assess the utility of different models in the context of \ac{sdg} systems.

We view \ac{sdg} as a promising research direction with applications extending beyond LLM-based facilitation. For example, it could support the study of state-sponsored influence campaigns using LLM agents to shape public narratives, spread misinformation, or promote propaganda. It may also enable the synthesis of expert-like opinions in a structured and interpretable form for human evaluation.

Several extensions of this work remain open. A dimorphic setup that separates participants and facilitators (\S\ref{sec:scalability:experimental}) could provide additional modeling flexibility, while the use of non-finetuned models may yield more authentic dialogue generation (\S\ref{sec:scalability:model}). Additionally, more sophisticated turn-taking mechanisms that grant greater agency to LLM participants could improve the realism and interactivity of simulated discussions (\S\ref{sec:methodology:management:turn-taking}).

Finally, although we find that facilitation strategies significantly affect LLM facilitator behavior, understanding the precise nature of these effects will require further validation through replication studies with human participants.


\subsection{Ethical considerations}
Synthetic discussions involving LLMs could be exploited by malicious actors to train them at performing unethical tasks \cite{majumdar_2024_nefarious,MARULLI20245340,li_2025_vulnerable}. Additionally, our research includes elements of LLM jail-breaking using conversational context, which could be used to poison discussions using safety-aligned models, although ongoing research is addressing these vulnerabilities \cite{wang_2025_risk}. Furthermore,the use of LLMs inherently risks skewing moderation systems towards the predominant demographics best represented in their training data. \ac{sdb} prompts are a necessary but insufficient step towards avoiding this \cite{rossi_2024,anthis_2025,burton2024large}.
