Advances in generative \ac{ai} technologies have enabled the study of human behaviors through Large Language Models (LLMs). Since these models are pretrained on extensive human discussion data, researchers have hypothesized that they could replicate certain human behaviors in social science experiments concerning behavioral social science studies~\cite{grossman_2023, tornberg_2023, argyle2023} or human-algorithm interaction~\cite{korre2025evaluation, cho-etal-2024-language, park_simulacra}, usually by creating synthetic simulation platforms~\cite{park_simulacra,tornberg_2023,y_social, zhou_2024_sotopia, chuang-etal-2024-simulating, li_2025_vulnerable, balog_2024}. However, since the emergent properties of LLMs have only been discovered relatively recently, studies using synthetic agents so far have employed various ad-hoc methodologies to define, run, and evaluate simulated discussion experiments~\cite{mou-etal-2024-unveiling, park_simulacra, park_2023_game, tornberg_2023, abdelnabi_negotiations, ulmer2024, park2024generativeagentsimulations1000}. In an attempt to consolidate the currently fragmented research landscape of synthetic simulations, we are the first to identify and define this, so far implicit, research direction:

\begin{definition}
	\textbf{\acf{sdg}}: Simulating textual human discussions, by using synthetic participants, ultimately aiming to understand and extract representative insights for human discussions.
\end{definition}

\ac{sdg} is a specific case of \acf{gabm}, which is a computational approach that simulates the interactions of agents to study complex behaviors and emergent phenomena. Unlike generalized \ac{gabm} systems, \ac{sdg} actors do not have a predetermined set of actions and behaviors; rather, both are expressed through free text. \ac{sdg} is typically used as a means to solve research questions in other tasks (called \emph{`downstream tasks'} in this study) where both human participation and a sustained conversational context are needed, as opposed to single exchanges (in which case, simple prompting would be sufficient). Examples include negotiations~\cite{abdelnabi_negotiations}, social experiments~\cite{park_simulacra} and human persona replication~\cite{park2024generativeagentsimulations1000}. Despite the breadth of downstream tasks, we argue that a well-designed \ac{sdg} methodology can generalize over most such tasks.

The lack of a unified design methodology in studies using \ac{sdg} results in greater difficulty in creating, running and assessing the validity of these experiments. Furthermore, \emph{there has been no study that addresses how to systematically create, evaluate, and use generalizable LLM simulations of discussions to solve research questions} without the need for human participants and evaluators. In this study, we answer the following research question (RQ):

\begin{research-question}
	How do we design and evaluate scalable\footnote{Scalable in this context implies that the system can maintain consistent performance and quality as the scale of the experiments increases, without requiring disproportionate increases in computational resources or financial cost.} SDG simulations that (a) replicate known representative behaviors found in real-life human discussions, and (b) can be used to obtain further insights on real-world phenomena?
\end{research-question}

We begin by creating a theoretical, downstream-task-agnostic framework for \ac{sdg}. Since there are no standards or guidelines on how to create \ac{sdg} systems, we examine task-specific systems in the literature and use lessons from \acf{swe} to derive design rules (\S\ref{sec:methodology:design}). Through this analysis, we formalize \acf{sdg} as a distinct research direction and identify four core design principles for effective synthetic simulations. We break down the requirements for \ac{sdg} and derive a set of isolated components that must be implemented and separately evaluated (\S\ref{sec:methodology:initialization},~\S\ref{sec:methodology:management}), including the construction of LLM personas and the execution of dynamic discussions. To assess whether observed behaviors are representative of real-world patterns, we build an evaluation suite grounded in \ac{gabm} literature and adapted for \ac{sdg}, which we use to verify the necessity and integrity of these components (\S\ref{sec:methodology:evaluation}). Our findings show that persona-driven simulations improve the diversity of generated content, aligning it more closely with human discussions, while careful instruction prompting can induce persistently toxic behavior in safety-aligned LLMs using only conversational context. Additionally, we observe that LLM agents tend not to abstain from participation, showcasing an inherent limitation in modeling realistic conversational dynamics.

Having created a theoretical \ac{sdg} framework, we tackle the issue of scalability: how can we minimize the required cost for running simulated experiments? Most recent studies~\cite{abdelnabi_negotiations, park_simulacra, park_2023_game, balog_2024, tornberg_2023} use proprietary models such as the OpenAI GPT family. We argue that the use of such models increases inference costs, which inherently limits the amount, efficiency, and scalability of experiments conducted using these models in real-world problems (\S\ref{sec:scalability:cost}). Indeed, we show that small (7B-8B parameter) quantized models are capable enough for our purposes (Finding~\ref{finding:model}) and able to replicate several social dynamics observed in human discussions (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:behaviors}) at a fraction of the cost. By developing a methodology for cost comparison between experiments with humans, proprietary models, and locally hosted LLMs (\S\ref{sec:scalability:cost}), we estimate that our approach using smaller models achieves a \emph{cost reduction of 1,600 times} compared to experiments with humans and \emph{44 times} compared to a recent, mid-capability proprietary model (GPT-5.1--see Table~\ref{tab:cost}).

Next, we investigate whether such simulations can produce insights transferrable to human discussions, by using LLM facilitation as a downstream task (\S\ref{sec:facilitation}). Platform designers and researchers have traditionally focused on identifying, flagging, and removing problematic content, an approach often called ``content moderation'' in literature~\cite{seering_self_moderation,cresci_pesonalized_interventions}. However, fully automatic content moderation is insufficient in practice~\cite{horta_automated_moderation, schaffner_community_guidelines, small-polis-llm, korre2025evaluation}. A more effective approach is using human facilitators,\footnote{Some publications use the term ``moderator'' with the meaning we have assigned to ``facilitator''~\cite{korre2025evaluation}.} who actively participate in a discussion. Still, this approach cannot be used scalably in modern social media sites where hundreds of thousands of discussions are being conducted each day. LLMs have been proposed as scalable facilitators~\cite{korre2025evaluation, small-polis-llm}, yet experimentation and development remain costly and constrained by the need for human discussants~\cite{rossi_2024, small-polis-llm}. The main issue is that facilitation cannot be meaningfully evaluated in isolated, single-turn listen-and-respond settings; it requires sustained conversational context~\cite{cho-etal-2024-language}, with multiple human participants actively conversing with the LLM facilitator. Our study is the first to use multiple participants to evaluate a LLM facilitator, and the first to use synthetic participants in lieu of humans. 

While the use of LLM participants and the relatively limited recent work on facilitation constrain the scope of our findings, our experiments uncover generalizable results. Specifically, we identify a severe and previously unreported limitation: LLM facilitators cannot choose not to intervene, even when intervention is unnecessary (Finding~\ref{finding:intervention}). This inability to remain silent has not, to our knowledge, been documented in prior literature. This behavior has been observed to induce frustration and toxicity among human participants in online communities~\cite{korre2025evaluation}. \footnote{A pattern we replicate in our experiments (App.~\ref{sec:appendix:behaviors}; Table~\ref{tab:logs-constant-intervention}).} Most importantly, failing to uncover this issue could lead researchers to invest significant time and resources in human-subject studies and complex LLM configurations~\cite{korre2025evaluation,small-polis-llm}, only to find their results fundamentally undermined. 
We also identify two real-life facilitation strategies and two common setups used in current LLM facilitation systems and through our experiments confirm that LLM facilitators are able to alter conversational dynamics according to different facilitation strategies (Finding~\ref{finding:strategy}).

Finally, we release an open-source Python framework\footnote{\librarylink} that implements our methodology at scale (\S\ref{sec:materials:software}), enabling the research community to rapidly experiment with synthetically generated discussions. Given that existing facilitation datasets are few and generally small \cite{korre2025evaluation}, we also release a large, publicly available dataset with LLM-generated synthetic discussions (\S\ref{sec:materials:data}). Our dataset can be used for further analysis of the behavior of out-of-the-box LLMs in synthetic discussions generally, and as LLM facilitators specifically, as well as for LLM facilitator fine-tuning \cite{ulmer2024} in future work. We use open-source LLMs and include all relevant configurations in order to make our study as reproducible as possible.
