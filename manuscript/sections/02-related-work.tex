\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Substituting human subjects with LLMs}
\label{sec:related:human-llm}

\ac{sdg} can be viewed as a specialization of \ac{gabm}, which uses generative agents to simulate believable human behavior~\cite{park_simulacra}. Early approaches to such simulations relied on rules-based and \ac{ml}-driven models~\cite{epstein1999_abms}, and have successfully demonstrated the emergence and replication of complex social dynamics across a range of scenarios~\cite{Schelling1971,reynold_boids}. In this context, LLMs can similarly act as agents within controlled simulated environments, extending the capabilities of prior approaches. LLMs can naturally exhibit bias and forms of irrationality in their behavior, which constituted a fundamental limitation of earlier models that tended to enforce overly rational decision-making~\cite{adornetto2025_gabms}.

\ac{sdg} systems include synthetic clones of Reddit \cite{park_simulacra}, Twitter/X \cite{mou-etal-2024-unveiling}, generic social media sites \cite{tornberg_2023,y_social}, games \cite{park_2023_game}, and social experiments \cite{zhou_2024_sotopia, chuang-etal-2024-simulating,li-etal-2024-econagent}. One approach~\cite{balog_2024} extracts topics and comments from online human discussions and prompts an LLM to recreate them based on summaries. Another approach~\cite{ulmer2024} creates synthetic discussions between two roles: an agent controlling a fictional environment and another client-agent interacting with it. These discussions are filtered and used to finetune the agent LLM for a specific task. Finally, a third approach  \cite{abdelnabi_negotiations} generates synthetic negotiations involving multiple agents with different agendas and responsibilities. Our experiments can be viewed as a domain shift from negotiation to discussion facilitation, where various user types (e.g., normal users, trolls) engage in discussion overseen by an actor with veto power (in our case, the facilitator).


Recent work \cite{grossman_2023, tornberg_2023,argyle2023} argues that synthetic agents have the potential to eventually replace human participants in social science studies. Indeed, LLMs have demonstrated complex, emergent social behaviors. In a simulated setting, LLM actors have been successful in diffusing information through social interaction, as well as planning and organizing spontaneous social events without human interaction \cite{park_2023_game}. In settings where LLM actors represent distinct factions with goals and agendas, they were shown to execute basic social strategies and adapt their plans in response to the other LLM actors \cite{abdelnabi_negotiations}. Additionally, substituting \acp{sdb} with information from personal interviews enables LLMs to reliably predict further survey responses from interviewed humans~\cite{park2024generativeagentsimulations1000}. %While this finding indicates that LLMs can extrapolate data points relating to human preferences, this approach is not usable in experimental settings at scale, since it relies on conducting extensive human interviews which are fed in their entirety to the models for each inference call.

However, significant limitations of LLMs remain in the context of Social Science experiments. Issues include undetectable behavioral hallucinations \cite{rossi_2024}; sociodemographic, statistical and political biases \cite{anthis_2025,hewitt2024predicting,rossi_2024,Taubenfeld2024SystematicBI}; unreliable annotations \cite{jansen_2023,bisbee_2023,neumann_2025,Gligoric2024CanUL}; non-deterministic outputs \cite{atil_2025,bisbee_2023}; and excessive agreeableness \cite{park_2023_game,anthis_2025,rossi_2024}. Even if LLMs can mimic some human behaviors, they are ultimately at most a statistical estimate of the ``average'' human encountered in their training data~\cite{rossi_2024}; thus we can not claim that any LLM behavior can be indicative of human behavioral patterns without extensive replication studies~\cite{rossi_2024} or system evaluation~\cite{adornetto2025_gabms}. This is a core, unavoidable limitation of any LLM simulation. Rather than asserting that the findings from our experiments will be directly replicable in human studies, we focus on reporting results that are likely to be applicable to real-world settings, based on thorough evaluation.



\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{sec:related:quality}


\subsubsection{Synthetic data quality}
\label{sec:related:quality:diversity}

When creating \acp{sdg} we need to evaluate \emph{synthetic data quality}, as discussions without human involvement tend to deteriorate quickly, often becoming repetitive and low-quality \cite{ulmer2024}. Despite the need for such metrics, methods for quantifying the quality of synthetic data remain limited~\cite{ulmer2024}. 

One approach uses a mix of graph-based and lexical similarity metrics, many of which depend on human discussion datasets for comparison~\cite{balog_2024}. The authors also propose a loosely defined ``coherence'' score, which is LLM-annotated without theoretical grounding. Another approach assesses quality through post-discussion surveys with humans, and by measuring lexical diversity to approximate the variety of opinions expressed~\cite{kim_et_al_chatbot}. 

\emph{Diversity}~\cite{ulmer2024} is a metric applied to discussions that penalizes repeated text sequences between comments\footnote{Also called posts or, in older publications, dialogue turns.} of the same discussion using average pairwise ROUGE-L\footnote{ROUGE-L measures the similarity between two texts by identifying the longest sequence of words that appear in both in the same order.} \cite{lin-2004-rouge} scores. Low diversity points to pathological problems with model generation (e.g., LLMs repeating previous comments). On the other hand, extremely high diversity may point to a lack of interaction between participants; a discussion in which participants engage with each other will feature some lexical overlap (e.g., common terms, paraphrasing points of other participants). Specifically, diversity is defined as follows, where $N_d$ is the length (in comments) of discussion $d$, and $c(i, d)$ is the $i$th comment of discussion $d$. 
\[
\textit{div}(d) = 1 - \frac{2}{N_d(N_d-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N_d-1} \sum_{\substack{j=i+1}}^{N_d} \textit{ROUGE-L}(c(i,d), c(j,d))
\]

This metric inherits some limitations of ROUGE scores, but it remains computationally efficient, interpretable, and domain-agnostic. We argue that the use of exact-word-matching, inherited from ROUGE, is not necessarily a disadvantage in \ac{sdg}. In fact, this approach provides the most direct and reliable way to detect ``collapsed discussions'' or model repetitions~\cite{ulmer2024}. Another advantage of this approach in \ac{sdg} is that humans frequently repeat specific phrases or quote exact portions of prior dialogue (`other-repetition') to respond to particular points, signal understanding, or summarize previous exchanges~\cite{cannava2016repertoire, svennevig2004other,rossi2020other}. Additionally, alternative metrics such as BLEU and BERTScore suffer from significant limitations in both practical applicability and reproducibility, despite the latter not relying on exact-word-matching~\cite{post-2018-call,chen-etal-2022-reproducibility, hanna-bojar-2021-fine}. For these reasons, we incorporate diversity in our analysis while acknowledging that more robust synthetic quality measures are still required.

%While  Diversity is sufficient for capturing variety in synthetic discussions, it is not enough to prove that the system is useful for research purposes. To prove this, we need to establish that a sufficient set of dynamics observed in the system are realistic~\cite{adornetto2025_gabms}. We discuss which evaluation methods are available to us for \ac{sdg} in the next section.


\subsubsection{GABM evaluation}
\label{sec:related:quality:gabm}

When evaluating \ac{sdg} systems, a naive assumption would be to test whether the text itself is ``believable'', which could be evaluated by tasking humans with discriminating between synthetic and human-written discussions~\cite{park_simulacra}. This approach is insufficient, since LLMs are explicitly trained to convincingly mimic human text, and because believable human text does not translate to believable social behaviors~\cite{rossi_2024}. We therefore turn to \ac{gabm} literature to find robust validation methods for \ac{sdg} systems.

There is currently no consensus on how a \ac{gabm} system should be evaluated~\cite{adornetto2025_gabms}. We can use automated methods developed for \emph{non-generative} agent-based models (foundational and empirical methods~\cite{collins2024}), or humans to judge observed behaviors and dynamics (face validation~\cite{rand2011_rigor}). Since the original procedures were designed with \ac{ml} and rule-based models in mind, a recent study~\cite{adornetto2025_gabms} proposes an adapted set of guidelines for generative agents, which we briefly outline below.


\paragraph{Foundational and Empirical Methods}
Foundational methods ensure data integrity and consistency with established benchmarks, without requiring manual evaluation:

\begin{itemize}[nosep, noitemsep]
	\item \textbf{Data analytics}: A prerequisite for all further analysis. Includes rigorous cleaning, organization, and statistical analysis to ensure data integrity.
	
	\item \textbf{Docking}: Also known as 'model-to-model comparison', this involves comparing a simulation's outputs with those of other state-of-the-art ABMs to verify alignment. It is particularly useful when a recognized reference model exists.
	
	\item \textbf{Empirical validation}: This strategy uses real-world historical or observational data to ensure that model predictions are grounded in reality. It is the primary means of evaluating a model's accuracy in replicating known phenomena.
	
	\item \textbf{Sampling}: Sampling involves exploring the simulation space using various techniques to understand how changes in input variables (e.g., decision thresholds) impact outcomes.
	
	\item \textbf{Visualization}: This family of methods utilizes graphical representations to analyze emergent behaviors and patterns. While powerful for detecting anomalies, it is subject to qualitative interpretation biases.
\end{itemize}


\paragraph{Face-Validation Methods}
Face-validation focuses on whether model processes and outcomes appear ``reasonable'' to humans. It is especially critical for \acp{gabm}, where natural language and social dynamics are central. Unlike empirical methods, face-validation does not strictly require external datasets, but does require manual  labor.

\begin{itemize}[nosep, noitemsep]
	\item \textbf{Survey-based validation}: Human evaluators, often dozens or hundreds, are engaged to rank or score agent behaviors based on their perceived believability and social intelligence.  An alternative interpretation suggests that the agents themselves could be ``interviewed'', and their responses be judged by humans~\cite{adornetto2025_gabms}.
	
	\item \textbf{Observational studies}: These methods analyze human behavior in real-world settings to compare them with generative agents. An example would be a study evaluating synthetic social media simulations by comparing generated responses to documented posts during a real-life disaster in Japan~\cite{gao2025_s3}. While empirical validation (see above) uses aggregate statistics from humans, these methods analyze behaviors on a higher level of abstraction.
	
	\item \textbf{Expert reviews}: Domain experts conduct structured walkthroughs or immersive assessments where they may even adopt the perspective of an agent to directly evaluate the coherence and plausibility of interactions. This approach has been used in work relating to the effectiveness of LLM facilitators~\cite{cho-etal-2024-language}, but this was made possible by the particular setup used, the relatively low number of samples, and paying participants relatively low wages.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Online Facilitation}
\label{sec:related:facilitation}

\subsubsection{Human Facilitation}

The modern social media environment has evolved to be extremely hostile, with users facing ever-increasing threats such as targeted misinformation \cite{clemons2025disinformation,Denniss2025Social}, hate speech \cite{kolluri2025parler}, and polarization \cite{pranesh2024impactsocialmediapolarization}. These threats can cause serious emotional and mental harm \cite{proactive_moderation}, radicalization \cite{cho-etal-2024-language}, real-world violence \cite{schaffner_community_guidelines}, they may sabotage democratic dialogue \cite{esau2017design,falk-etal-2021-predicting,seering_self_moderation}, and undermine trust in democratic institutions \cite{schroeder-etal-2024-fora}. Threats of these kinds have been greatly amplified with the advent of LLMs; malicious actors can use generative \ac{ai} technology to mass-produce misinformation~\cite{kreps2023ai, summerfield2024will}, attack online infrastructure~\cite{summerfield2024will}, and overwhelm online communities with synthetic content~\cite{saura2025synthetification}. On the other hand, evidence shows that human moderators experience elevated levels of psychological distress, including secondary trauma~\cite{spence2024content}, compassion fatigue, vicarious trauma and burnout~\cite{spence2023psychological}, as well as exhaustion~\cite{steiger2021psychological}, with symptoms worsening through prolonged exposure~\cite{spence2024content}.

In light of the limitations of traditional content moderation, researchers have increasingly turned to approaches in which moderators actively engage with users through dialogue--often termed conversational moderation or facilitation~\cite{argyle2023, korre2025evaluation, falk-etal-2021-predicting, cho-etal-2024-language}. Rather than reacting to violations after they occur, facilitation aims to preempt problematic behavior~\cite{cho-etal-2024-language,seering_self_moderation,cresci_pesonalized_interventions,make_reddit_great} while fostering deliberation and collective decision-making within communities~\cite{kim_et_al_chatbot,seering_self_moderation}. Human facilitators play a crucial role in online communities, serving as key contributors to maintaining social cohesion, encouraging participation, and guiding discussions in a constructive manner~\cite{bcause2023}. Their role is often informal and community-driven, with facilitators typically selected by the community members themselves~\cite{bcause2023} and functioning as de-facto community leaders~\cite{seering_self_moderation}. As such, they are not only moderators in the traditional sense but also volunteers who embody the values and norms of the group~\cite{korre2025evaluation}. The diversity of approaches to human moderation reflects the complexity of online interaction, with various strategies employed to manage discourse, resolve conflicts, and ensure equal participation~\cite{gao-etal-2025-moderation,schroeder-etal-2024-fora,chen-etal-2025-whow, park_et_al_2012_facilitation}. 


\subsubsection{LLM Facilitators}
Human facilitators face many of the same psychological challenges as content moderators~\cite{schopke2024volunteer}, and cannot handle the sheer scale of modern-day social network traffic~\cite{schaffner_community_guidelines}, limiting the scalability of purely human-driven facilitation approaches. Given the scale of threats to online communities and the growing capacity to mass-produce harmful content, reliance on a predominantly human-centered moderation model is increasingly untenable; we must thus turn to automated tools~\cite{small-polis-llm, korre2025evaluation}. Unlike classification models often used in online platforms to detect content violating norms, LLMs can actively facilitate discussions \cite{korre2025evaluation}. 
They can warn users for rule violations \cite{Kumar_AbuHashem_Durumeric_2024}, monitor engagement \cite{schroeder-etal-2024-fora}, aggregate diverse opinions \cite{small-polis-llm}, and provide translations and writing tips, which are especially useful for marginalized groups \cite{Tsai2024Generative}. These capabilities suggest that LLMs may be able to assist or even replace human facilitators in many tasks \cite{small-polis-llm,korre2025evaluation}.

Despite the potential of LLMs in this role, very few studies have actually implemented and evaluated LLM facilitators. One approach used simple rule-based models~\cite{kim_et_al_chatbot} and reported promising results in organizing user activity. Another study used LLM facilitators to privately discuss with a single human who pretended to be a participant in a by-then-archived discussion~\cite{cho-etal-2024-language}. In contrast to both works, our experiments use multiple exclusively LLM participants and LLM facilitators, evaluating the latter within an explicitly toxic and challenging environment. A third approach uses LLM participants, trolls, and moderators with \acp{sdb} in a synthetic clone of Reddit~\cite{park_simulacra}. While our experimental setups are similar, our study (1) attempts to create a generalizable framework instead of a task-specific experimental setup, (2) uses local models instead of proprietary models used in the study, (3) provides a robust and scalable evaluation setup instead of human annotators discriminating between synthetic and human-authored text (see \S\ref{sec:related:quality} for why this evaluation setup is insufficient, and \S\ref{sec:methodology:evaluation} for scalability concerns).
